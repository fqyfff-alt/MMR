03 Comparative Analysis

Why SSR, and not InSAR or LiDAR

Three slope-monitoring technologies share the same goal. Only one matches the scan frequency and precision a working quarry like Ibri's actually needs.

Comparison compiled from Denis (2026) and Le Roux et al. (2025). See full sources.
Technology How it works Accuracy Scan frequency
SSR Ground-based radar unit measuring rock-face movement at ranges up to 5.6 km Sub-millimetre Every 2–40 minutes
InSAR Satellite-based, monitoring on the same underlying principle as SSR — wider area coverage 1–3 mm Every 6–12 days
LiDAR Pulses of light reflected off the rock face, generating accurate 3D scans Millimetre-level —

Scroll horizontally to see all columns

Quarries require constant monitoring to ensure worker safety — so this project proposes SSR over InSAR or LiDAR. SSR's sub-millimetre accuracy is superior to both.

SSR

Ground-based Slope Stability Radar. Sub-millimetre accuracy at ranges of up to 5.6 km, scanning every 2–40 minutes — built for constant, close monitoring of a single working slope.

InSAR

Interferometric Synthetic Aperture Radar. Satellite-based and operating on essentially the same principles as SSR, offering a wider view of the quarry — but only 1–3 mm accuracy and a scan once every 6–12 days.

LiDAR

Light Detection and Ranging. Uses pulses of light that reflect off objects to generate accurate 3D scans of rock faces and detect motion or cracks, at millimetre-level accuracy.

Sources: Denis (2026); Le Roux et al. (2025); Reeves et al. (2001). See full sources.

Proven elsewhere

International deployments

SSR is not experimental — it is already at work monitoring rock faces on several continents.

Australia

Open-cut mine deployments monitoring and warning before slope incidents.

Indonesia

Deployed to monitor mine sites and provide early warning ahead of failures.

South Africa

SSR combined with InSAR for long-term deformation detection at the Gamsberg Mine.

China

Radar-linked monitoring documented at the Xinmo quarry.

Sources: Harries, Noon, & Rowley (2006); SkyGeo / Gamsberg Mine (n.d.); Tordesillas et al. (2021). See full sources.
